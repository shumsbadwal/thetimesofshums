Economists forecast 7.6% Indian GDP growth; it is not yet an official result

A Reuters poll of 43 economists estimates that India’s real GDP expanded 7.6% from a year earlier in July–September 2026, after reported growth of 7.8% in April–June. The forecast reflects resilient consumer demand despite inflation and geopolitical pressures. The official release was still pending on the edition date.

Fast GDP growth can coexist with financial strain for households if wage gains lag prices or borrowing supports consumption. The shape of growth—jobs, wages, sectoral productivity and the distribution of gains—matters as much as the headline rate. The forecast should not be presented as a confirmed statistic.

India’s inflation breaches the RBI’s upper tolerance limit

India’s consumer-price inflation rose to 6.09% in October from 5.54% in September, breaching the Reserve Bank of India’s 6% upper tolerance limit. Food inflation reached 8.94%, according to the official figures reported by Business Standard. The increase follows pressures from food supplies and earlier energy-price moves; inflation does not imply every product rose by the same amount.

The bigger picture is a monetary-policy dilemma. India is growing rapidly, yet households experience purchasing-power losses when food and essentials outpace wages. Higher policy rates might help contain broad inflation but also raise borrowing costs for firms and consumers. The next RBI decision is due on 4 December. One month outside the target band does not establish that all price pressures are permanent, but it increases the importance of the next inflation prints and the distinction between food-driven and underlying inflation.

India's October merchandise trade deficit widens to $39.88 billion

India's goods-trade deficit reached $39.88 billion in October 2026, the highest in a year, widening from $30.21 billion in September, according to commerce data reported by Reuters. Imports rose particularly strongly, including gold, while the government also reported a record month for merchandise exports. The trade balance refers to goods, not India's full current account including services.

The numbers reveal the tension between an expanding economy and its dependence on imported energy, precious metals and other goods. Higher exports can coexist with a wider deficit if imports grow faster. Whether the gap persists will depend on commodity prices, domestic demand and service export receipts.

Business and Finance - IndiaIndia · Labour marketStory 4 of 10

India’s unemployment rate edges down, with more women in the workforce

India’s monthly Periodic Labour Force Survey recorded a 4.9% unemployment rate in October, down from 5.0% in September. Female labour-force participation rose to 35.0% from 34.6%, and urban unemployment also eased, according to reporting on figures released 16 November. These are survey estimates subject to sampling and definition, not a count of every person seeking work.

The bigger picture is the quality, security and accessibility of work, not a tenth-of-a-point change by itself. A lower unemployment rate may coexist with low earnings, informal work or underemployment. Higher female participation is encouraging but does not automatically establish progress in pay, hours, workplace safety or career opportunities. The next useful comparisons are urban–rural differences, the types of jobs being created and whether participation gains persist.

Business and Finance - IndiaCurrency · MarketsStory 5 of 10

Rupee gives back part of a seven-month high as the dollar strengthens

The Indian rupee closed at 93.44 to the US dollar on 18 November, near the previous session's 93.47, after giving back part of its recent gains, Reuters reported via Moneycontrol. The broader dollar strengthened as traders weighed US interest-rate expectations. These quoted levels are a daily close, not a forecast.

Currency movements feed into the rupee cost of imported fuel, foreign-currency borrowing and overseas spending, while also influencing exporters' receipts. A one-day move should not be overinterpreted: capital flows, intervention, oil prices and global rates all pull in different directions.

Business and Finance - IndiaIndia · Capital flowsStory 6 of 10

Foreign investors return to Indian equities in record November inflows

Foreign portfolio investors purchased about $9.53 billion of Indian stocks in November through 16 November, Reuters reported—the highest monthly inflow recorded in its dataset even before the month ended. The move reversed earlier selling and accompanied a strong rally in banking, financial and other domestic shares as oil prices moderated and investors reassessed heavily concentrated global AI holdings.

The bigger picture is that flows into emerging markets can respond quickly to global interest rates, currency expectations and relative stock-market valuations. Foreign buying can support the rupee and share prices but is not proof that every company has become fundamentally more valuable. A strong monthly inflow can reverse if global risk appetite changes. It is more useful to watch whether domestic earnings and credit conditions justify the re-rating than to interpret a market surge as an economic verdict.

Business and Finance - IndiaIndia · Markets and regulationStory 7 of 10

SEBI considers guardrails as leveraged share purchases grow

India’s securities regulator is reviewing the margin trading facility through which investors buy shares using money borrowed from brokers. Reuters reported that margin-funded exposure has risen sharply, with some brokers offering up to four times investors’ capital at interest rates of 9–18%. On 18 November SEBI chair Tuhin Kanta Pandey said no decision had been made on the proposals.

The concern is that concentrated borrowing can amplify sell-offs: if prices fall, margin calls can force sales, potentially deepening market volatility. Yet restricting leverage can also affect market liquidity and brokers’ business models. A possible safeguard linking positions to shares available for trading is being examined, not implemented. The significant news is regulatory consideration amid growing exposure, not a confirmed ban on margin funding.

Air India Express appoints its finance chief as CEO amid expansion

Air India Express named finance chief Deepak Rajawat as its chief executive on 18 November, according to Reuters. The budget carrier is preparing for its next phase of growth within the wider Air India group. The appointment is a corporate announcement, not an independent assessment of the airline's future performance.

Low-cost airlines face a demanding combination of fleet investment, fuel prices, staffing, on-time operations and fare competition. A leadership change may shape priorities, but passengers and investors will ultimately judge reliability, safety, network choices and financial outcomes.

Business and Finance - IndiaIndia · Banking and acquisitionsStory 9 of 10

Fairfax sells its IIFL Finance holding as its IDBI Bank deal takes shape

Canada’s Fairfax Financial sold its 13.64% stake in IIFL Finance through a block transaction worth roughly ₹34.54 billion ($370 million), according to a person with knowledge of the transaction cited by Reuters on 18 November. Reuters linked the planned exit to fundraising for Fairfax’s agreed purchase of a 60% stake in IDBI Bank from government-related sellers, a deal valued at around ₹528.78 billion.

The broader significance is foreign investment in Indian banking. Rather than building entirely new retail networks, overseas investors are buying established institutions with licenses, deposits and customers. That can bring capital and competition, but also raises questions about governance, integration and the strategic direction of privatised banking assets. The IIFL transaction was attributed to a source; the final use of sale proceeds and implementation details should not be assumed beyond reported plans.

Scotch import-duty dispute tests the operation of the India–UK trade agreement

Reuters reported that India planned to impose duty and tax totalling 162.5% on certain bulk Scotch imports amid a dispute over tariff classification under the India–UK trade arrangement. Indian officials had earlier said they would honour the agreement's concessions. This concerns a specific import category and compliance interpretation, not all British goods or all Scotch sales.

The issue matters to importers because tariff rates determine costs, pricing and commercial certainty. It also illustrates how trade agreements depend on detailed customs rules long after headline deals are signed. Reported planned treatment and industry objections should not be conflated with a final adjudication of alleged misclassification.
