CDR & Marine CDROAE · ecologyStory 1 of 8

Zooplankton study tests ecological responses to ocean alkalinity enhancement

A 54-day Norwegian mesocosm study published in ICES Journal of Marine Science evaluated lime-inspired and silicate-inspired alkalinity treatments, up to a 600 μmol/kg increase in total alkalinity. Its authors reported limited overall changes in several measures of zooplankton and food-web functioning under the tested conditions, alongside some more specific biological responses.

For mCDR, the lesson is neither that OAE is harmless nor that ecological damage is inevitable. Material chemistry, dosing, dilution, local ecology, nutrients and season can all alter risk. Mesocosm findings cannot settle repeated exposure or large-scale field impacts. The study helps refine the questions that environmental-monitoring plans should test.

CDR & Marine CDRDirect ocean captureStory 2 of 8

Mussel experiments flag discharge risks for direct ocean capture

Researchers exposed marine mussels to low-dissolved-inorganic-carbon, high-pH water representative of possible direct ocean carbon capture and storage discharge conditions. Changes in oxygen consumption and spawning responses were reported under some treatments, with recovery observed for some indicators after exposure ended.

The project-level question is not simply whether mussels survive a short experiment. Sublethal stress, shell-closing behaviour, reproductive effects and repeated exposure could matter near discharge points. This suggests that mixing design, concentration control and ecological monitoring will be important for electrochemical mCDR projects. The experiment does not quantify a net atmospheric tonne removed.

CDR & Marine CDRWastewater · OAEStory 3 of 8

Wastewater-based OAE shows carbonate-chemistry changes, not verified removal

An Environmental Research study examined olivine-assisted alkalinisation of wastewater before mixing it with seawater. Researchers reported a pH increase in the treated effluent and, after mixing, roughly 140 μmol/kg additional seawater alkalinity and a 0.1 pH-unit increase. The experiment also investigated responses in coastal microbial communities.

Existing wastewater infrastructure could offer a convenient deployment interface, but wastewater chemistry, contaminants and discharge permits create distinct risks. Most importantly, the measured chemistry changes do not establish a net quantity of atmospheric CO₂ uptake. Creditable removal would also require air–sea exchange estimates, leakage and lifecycle accounting, baselines and uncertainty analysis.

CDR & Marine CDROAE · thresholdsStory 4 of 8

North Sea OAE experiments show why local dose and exposure matter

A Communications Earth & Environment study of spring phytoplankton blooms found relatively small effects at lower tested alkalinity changes but delays of about six to nine days at higher doses around or above 900 μmol/kg. The findings depend on the study’s experimental conditions and cannot be converted into one universal regulatory threshold.

Bloom timing can alter the availability of food and nutrients within marine ecosystems. For project design, the significance is that the same total alkalinity addition can create different exposure patterns depending on mixing, dilution and season. Local discharge limits and MRV protocols need to consider where chemistry changes occur, not only the total amount of material added.

CDR & Marine CDRGovernanceStory 5 of 8

IMO parties adopt research-assessment frameworks for marine geoengineering

At their October meeting, parties to the London Convention and London Protocol adopted frameworks to assess scientific research involving ocean fertilisation, ocean alkalinity enhancement and biomass sinking. The frameworks aim to evaluate whether research proposals may have unacceptable marine impacts.

This is progress towards consistent research scrutiny, not a blanket licence for commercial deployment or a carbon-crediting standard. Applicable domestic law and international obligations still matter. As field trials grow, developers will need robust project-specific environmental assessments alongside separate methods for demonstrating net durable removal.

Desalination co-location gains interest as a possible mCDR pathway

Specialist reporting says French marine CDR developer Pronoe is exploring commercial expansion of a demonstration at a Spanish desalination facility, with an ambition to issue its first credits within about a year. This is a reported development plan, not verified delivery of atmospheric removals or issued credits.

Desalination facilities already process seawater at scale and have intake and discharge infrastructure, which could lower some deployment costs. But brine chemistry, salinity, energy demand, permitting and discharge mixing all complicate environmental assessment. Credible project MRV would need to establish additional atmospheric uptake, permanence and net removals after lifecycle emissions—not infer credits merely from altered seawater chemistry.

CDR & Marine CDRCDR markets · Contracted vs deliveredStory 7 of 8

The durable CDR market shrinks in contracted volume while deliveries grow

CDR.fyi reported that purchases of durable carbon dioxide removal totalled 6.5 million tonnes in the first nine months of 2026, a 74% drop from the same period in 2025, largely reflecting much smaller Microsoft commitments after exceptional purchases the previous year. However, reported deliveries rose 13% to 458,000 tonnes. Excluding Microsoft and Frontier, new purchasing volume reached 4.2 million tonnes during the period.

The important distinction is between contracted tonnes, delivered tonnes and retired credits. Sales signal future financial commitments, while delivery and retirement are nearer evidence of actual execution, though credible permanence and MRV still depend on the applicable methodology. CDR.fyi’s figures describe the broader durable CDR market, dominated by bio-based pathways; they are not specific mCDR volumes. For emerging ocean-removal developers, the gap between buyer interest and verified delivery—and the timing of possible compliance demand—is a central commercial risk.

CDR & Marine CDRmCDR · MRV and deploymentStory 8 of 8

A new OAE review argues for locally designed projects, not universal carbon-removal assumptions

A perspective in Nature Reviews Earth & Environment published in October argues that ocean alkalinity enhancement should be understood as a collection of locally specific interventions rather than a single uniform ocean-scale technology. The authors highlight the importance of mixing and discharge conditions, sediment chemistry, mineral precipitation, trace metals, nutrient interactions and lifecycle emissions in determining risks and carbon-removal performance.

For mCDR, the key insight is that adding alkalinity does not guarantee an equivalent amount of additional atmospheric CO₂ uptake. Added alkalinity can alter natural processes, including carbonate dissolution and generation of background alkalinity, complicating additionality. Effective MRV will need local observations, defensible baselines and models that resolve air–sea exchange and uncertainty. This is a peer-reviewed perspective synthesising evidence and proposing an approach, not a newly verified field trial or a blanket approval for OAE deployment.
