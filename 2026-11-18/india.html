IndiaParliament · RepresentationStory 1 of 15

Women’s reservation and delimitation are back on the parliamentary agenda

Reporting ahead of Parliament’s expected winter sitting says the BJP-led government is considering another attempt to pass legislation linking implementation of the 33% women’s reservation law with a new delimitation exercise. The package under discussion could expand the Lok Sabha from 543 to as many as 850 seats, with the aim of making women’s reservation effective from the 2029 election. A previous attempt to advance the change failed to secure the constitutionally required majority in April. As of 18 November, a new special sitting remained a possibility reported by political sources, not an announced or completed legislative decision.

There are two distinct questions here: how quickly to increase women’s representation and how the expansion or redrawing of constituencies would affect each state’s weight in Parliament. Tamil Nadu’s assembly has urged implementation of the quota without enlarging the Lok Sabha, reflecting concern about redistribution of parliamentary influence. The Centre and some allies argue that expanding the House can accommodate new reserved seats and additional representation. Neither a timetable nor a formula should be treated as settled until legislation is introduced, debated and passed.

A constitutional amendment requires a majority of the total membership and at least two-thirds of members present and voting in each House, so coalition arithmetic and opposition positions matter. The story is as much about federal representation and the shape of India’s legislature as it is about the government’s parliamentary calendar.

IndiaUttar Pradesh · 2027 electionsStory 2 of 15

Congress and Samajwadi Party confront the arithmetic of an Uttar Pradesh alliance

The Congress leadership met its Uttar Pradesh organisation in Delhi on 16 November ahead of discussions with the Samajwadi Party over seat sharing for the state’s 2027 assembly election. The Indian Express reported Congress figures looking to contest more than 100 of the 403 seats; SP leader Akhilesh Yadav had referred to a much larger request of 181 seats. These are negotiating positions and descriptions by political figures, not a seat-sharing agreement.

Both parties participate in the opposition INDIA bloc but have different organisational strengths and ambitions in Uttar Pradesh. Congress wants room to rebuild its local cadre and candidates; the SP must weigh that against constituencies where it believes it has a stronger base. Agreements over candidate selection, campaigning and regional allies may matter as much as the eventual number allocated to each party.

Congress was also planning a Lucknow event around Constitution Day on 26 November. Announced rallies and negotiations are political preparations rather than an indication of how voters will behave next year. Reporting on the BJP and other parties’ plans will remain equally relevant as they become concrete.

IndiaCampaign finance · InvestigationStory 3 of 15

Election spending disclosures illuminate the companies behind political advertising

An investigation by Scroll examined Bharatiya Janata Party expenditure filings with the Election Commission for assembly elections in Bihar in 2025 and West Bengal, Kerala and Assam in 2026. The report says a firm trading as CNX Media received about ₹28.5 crore for media advertising, placing it behind Google but ahead of Meta among the prominent recipients identified in the party’s filings. The BJP separately reported approximately ₹530 crore in campaign spending across those elections, according to the investigation.

The figures offer a view of the commercial infrastructure of an election campaign: media buying, agencies, platforms and intermediaries are not necessarily visible to voters who see the final advertisements. The amounts are derived from declared spending categories; they do not independently prove which messages were seen, their effects on voting, or any legal breach.

For public scrutiny, the next questions are how contracts are awarded, whether firms’ ownership and services can be traced, and how campaign spending disclosures capture advertising that travels through multiple intermediaries. Examining expenditure across parties and election cycles is necessary before drawing broad conclusions about comparative behaviour.

IndiaKarnataka · Government accountabilityStory 4 of 15

Karnataka’s publicity bill raises questions about the boundary between information and promotion

Newslaundry examined Karnataka government advertising and publicity expenditure using legislative replies and records obtained under the Right to Information Act. It reported that the Congress-led state government spent about ₹473.46 crore across the financial years 2023–24, 2024–25 and 2025–26, equivalent to roughly ₹43.24 lakh per day over the period. The report compared this with about ₹331.54 crore spent across the corresponding three financial years of the preceding BJP government, a difference of roughly ₹142 crore.

Government advertising can have a legitimate public-information function, including awareness of health, education and welfare services. The accountability question is whether spending is proportionate, how media outlets and agencies are selected, and whether publicly funded material primarily informs residents or promotes political personalities. A difference in spending does not, by itself, establish misuse of funds.

This is also a media-policy story: substantial state advertising can influence the economics of newspapers and broadcasters. The underlying records, procurement criteria and content of the advertisements are better tests than party accusations alone.

IndiaWest Bengal · State policyStory 5 of 15

West Bengal signals changes to land ceiling and tenancy rules as it seeks investment

West Bengal minister Swapan Dasgupta told ThePrint that the state government intends to remove what he described as outdated land-ceiling and tenancy restrictions as part of an effort to encourage investment and industry. This is a statement of policy intention, not evidence that the legal changes have already been enacted or that a final bill has been published.

Land reform has a long and politically significant history in Bengal, including protections for cultivators and tenants. Supporters of deregulation argue that clearer rules can make it easier to assemble land, attract factories and invest in commercial projects. Critics and affected communities may ask whether the changes protect tenure, compensation and bargaining power, especially for smaller landholders and tenants.

The decisive questions are what the eventual legal text changes, how existing rights are handled, whether any consultations occur, and what independent evidence emerges about investment and land access. A ministerial announcement alone cannot settle those trade-offs.

IndiaCourts · Right to protestStory 6 of 15

Supreme Court seeks responses as traders challenge Delhi’s designated protest sites

On 18 November the Supreme Court sought responses from Congress, AAP and student organisations after traders’ associations asked that Ramlila Maidan no longer be designated a protest site. The traders argue that prolonged agitations near major commercial areas interfere with their businesses. Earlier proceedings also concerned the status of Jantar Mantar and the availability of alternative venues for demonstrations. Issuing notices is a procedural step; the court has not thereby accepted all the claims or made a final ruling on the venue.

The dispute involves competing constitutional and public interests. Political groups and citizens need meaningful locations in which to assemble and communicate dissent. Residents, traders and other users of public space also have interests in safety, mobility and uninterrupted livelihoods. Conditions on duration, routes and access are among the questions that such litigation can raise.

The broader issue is whether protest sites are practical and visible enough to serve democratic participation, while avoiding indefinite exclusion of everyone else. A court process addressing those rights should be reported separately from political claims made by the organisations involved.

IndiaPunjab · Political representationStory 7 of 15

Amritpal Singh’s brief custody parole puts Punjab politics and representation in focus

An Amritsar court authorised three hours of custody parole on 21 November for Khadoor Sahib MP Amritpal Singh to attend his grandmother’s cremation in Punjab. The order specifies his presence under custody rather than unrestricted release; he has been held in Assam’s Dibrugarh jail. ThePrint reported that the brief visit is attracting political attention because of his association with Waris Punjab De and the approaching state election.

The distinction between custody parole and general bail is important. The order addresses participation in a family ritual under stated conditions; it does not resolve the legal proceedings or wider questions surrounding his detention. He remains an elected MP, which also places the debate in the context of how constituents are represented when their parliamentarian is imprisoned.

Punjab’s AAP government, other state parties and Amritpal’s organisation have different political interests in the situation. A tightly restricted visit may carry symbolic significance, but it is not evidence of any future electoral outcome or a change in his legal status.

IndiaForeign policyStory 8 of 15

Quad leaders agree new maritime, AI and business initiatives

The leaders of India, the US, Japan and Australia met in Manila on 17 November. Their joint announcements included maritime surveillance cooperation, new technology initiatives and a Quad Business Leaders’ Forum planned for Mumbai in 2027. Some projects are still at the planning stage and will require implementation beyond the summit.

The Quad is evolving from a diplomatic forum towards practical coordination, especially around sea lanes, technology and supply-chain resilience. For India, participation strengthens ties with regional partners while preserving an independent foreign policy that still involves China and Russia. The wording of summit declarations matters, but tangible joint activities will be the real test of progress.

IndiaEnergyStory 9 of 15

India’s refiners appear to be shifting some purchases from Russia to Brazil

Preliminary shipment estimates reported by ThePrint show Russian crude imports could fall by nearly 400,000 barrels a day in November, while Brazilian deliveries could approach 400,000 barrels a day from about 100,000 in October. These are provisional monthly estimates, not a final measure of completed deliveries.

The change illustrates that crude sourcing is a commercial calculation involving discounts, tanker rates, refinery configurations and supply reliability, even though it is often cast primarily in diplomatic terms. Multiple suppliers can give India resilience, but a one-month shift does not prove a permanent strategic change. The next several months of actual imports will be more revealing.

IndiaJusticeStory 10 of 15

Supreme Court grants conditional bail to two Bilkis Bano case convicts

The Supreme Court suspended the sentences of two men convicted in the 2002 Bilkis Bano gang-rape and murder case while their appeals remain before the court. The men were granted conditional bail after spending more than 17 years in prison. Suspension of a sentence pending appeal is not an acquittal or a reversal of the convictions.

The case raises difficult questions about the rights of convicted people seeking appellate relief and the interests of victims in having a meaningful voice in proceedings affecting their security and the administration of justice. Reporting about legal standards should be distinguished from speculation about how the eventual appeals will be decided.

The Agnipath scheme may move towards longer service and more selective retention

Defence authorities are discussing revised arrangements under which Agniveers who are not immediately retained in regular service could receive an additional two-year extension following their first four years. Proposals also place greater emphasis on technical skills, discipline, fitness and competency-based selection. The details and timing remain unsettled.

The reforms go to the heart of military manpower policy: how to build experience, pay for personnel and give recruits credible post-service pathways. Longer service could improve training returns, but it also raises questions about career stability and what proportion eventually enter permanent posts. Plans under consideration should not be reported as an enacted scheme.

IndiaAgriculture · Food policyStory 12 of 15

India’s rice surplus is exposing the costs of agricultural subsidy policy

A Reuters investigation finds that India’s success in expanding rice production has produced large surpluses and growing financial and storage pressures. National rice output reached a reported 154 million tonnes in 2025/26, against domestic consumption of roughly 107 million tonnes. Rice subsidies account for more than two-thirds of the federal food-subsidy bill, which reached approximately ₹2.28 trillion in 2025/26. Officials and scientists interviewed by Reuters expressed concern that incentives continue to favour rice despite costs to public finances and other crops.

The policy dilemma is real: procurement and minimum support prices protect farm incomes and food security, but can also encourage water-intensive cultivation, pressure groundwater and crowd out pulses or oilseeds that India still imports. Export growth has created further tensions with rival producers. Reform cannot simply mean ending support for farmers; it would need to protect livelihoods while changing incentives, storage capacity and crop choices. The competing objectives make this a structural story, not just a record-harvest headline.

IndiaPublic health · Antibiotic resistanceStory 13 of 15

New surveillance data show antibiotic resistance across a wider range of Indian hospitals

An Indian Council of Medical Research surveillance report for 2025 highlights high resistance to several commonly used antibiotics among bacteria including E. coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae. Reporting by The Indian Express notes that the surveillance now includes data from 34 secondary-care hospitals, revealing serious resistance challenges beyond major tertiary centres. Some hospital-associated infections also show resistance to carbapenems, an important class of powerful antibiotics.

The larger significance is that ordinary infections can become harder and more expensive to treat when first-choice medicines stop working. Patterns vary by organism, drug, hospital and region: the report does not mean every infection is now untreatable or that a single antibiotic is ineffective everywhere. Improving laboratory diagnosis, prevention, hospital infection control and responsible prescribing are all needed. The findings come from an official surveillance network but are best understood through the reported methods and clinical limitations, not an institutional headline alone.

IndiaElections · Judicial scrutinyStory 14 of 15

Supreme Court to hear opposition questions about the electoral-roll revision process

A Supreme Court bench has deferred to 9 December consideration of an application by Rahul Gandhi and other opposition leaders seeking permission to put 48 formal questions to the Election Commission about the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) process. The opposition alleges irregularities and insufficient transparency; the Election Commission has challenged aspects of the application. The court has not, by postponing the hearing, accepted the opposition’s allegations or issued a final finding about the exercise.

The bigger issue is the standard of scrutiny applied to decisions about who appears on electoral rolls. Voter lists need updating, but erroneous exclusions can affect fundamental political participation. The case concerns access to information, review procedures and institutional accountability, distinct from Maharashtra’s separate administrative deadline extension. The December hearing may help clarify what evidence must be disclosed and what procedural safeguards are available.

IndiaCulture · InstitutionsStory 15 of 15

An investigation finds India’s leading visual-arts institution short of leadership and staff

A recent ground report by ThePrint describes deep administrative difficulties at the Lalit Kala Akademi, a major national institution supporting the visual arts. The report says its chairperson’s post has been vacant since April, its governing council is no longer functioning and nearly three-fifths of sanctioned staff positions are unfilled. It also describes a history of disputes and audit concerns. The reporting was published on 11 November, but the institutional problems it examines are ongoing.

The larger question is whether public cultural institutions have the governance capacity to carry out their mandates. Staffing vacancies and unclear decision-making can affect grants, exhibitions, support for artists and public accountability, even when they rarely make national headlines. Allegations from audits and the perspectives of involved officials require careful distinction from proven misconduct. Including this story is a deliberate choice to make room for consequential investigative reporting outside the daily political cycle.
