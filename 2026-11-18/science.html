Experimental leukaemia vaccine reports a survival benefit in Phase 3

SELLAS Life Sciences announced top-line Phase 3 findings for galinpepimut-S (GPS), an experimental therapeutic vaccine for a specific group of patients with acute myeloid leukaemia in remission after recurrent disease. In the trial, median overall survival was reported as 24.0 months with GPS versus 14.7 months with comparison treatment, with a hazard ratio of 0.59 (a 41% relative reduction in the hazard of death).

Therapeutic cancer vaccines seek to activate immune responses against cancer cells rather than prevent infection. These company-reported results are encouraging for the studied group, not a general cancer cure. Full data review, patient selection, safety assessment and regulatory consideration remain necessary. A relative hazard reduction should not be confused with a 41-percentage-point improvement in survival.

Chandrayaan-3 finds three distinct layers in the Moon’s upper soil

Analysis of ChaSTE instrument data from Chandrayaan-3’s Vikram lander indicates three thermal-property layers within roughly the upper 10 centimetres of lunar regolith. The top two to three centimetres appear extremely porous, around 80%, with different conductive properties below.

These measurements are important for understanding lunar geological history and designing equipment that must interact with or transfer heat through the surface. The inferred layers are based on thermophysical measurements and modelling, not photographs of a cutaway in the ground. Their exact origins remain a research question.

A mantle-convection model offers an explanation for missing geological time

A study in Nature explores the Great Unconformity, a widespread gap where young rocks sit directly on far older formations. The researchers model how vast continental regions could rise and subside over geological time as mantle convection reshaped Earth’s surface, affecting erosion and the preservation of rock layers.

The study connects deep-Earth processes to geological features observed across distant regions. It is a model-supported explanation, not a demonstration that glaciation, rifting or other forces played no role. This research was published earlier in November and earns a place because it addresses a long-standing geological puzzle.

OpenScientist illustrates both the promise and limits of AI-assisted research

A Nature Medicine paper introduces OpenScientist, an open agentic AI system that can read literature, formulate hypotheses and execute biomedical analyses. Its authors evaluated it in five specialist biomedical case studies and report substantial acceleration of some analysis workflows.

The interesting shift is from AI as a writing assistant towards systems that help organise scientific investigation. But a convincing generated hypothesis is not a clinical finding. Independent replication, scrutiny of data handling and experimental validation remain essential, especially when biomedical conclusions could eventually affect care.

CRISPR activation restores gene function in laboratory models of autism

Research published in Nature Neuroscience on 13 November used enhancer-targeted CRISPR activation to raise expression of two autism-associated genes, CHD8 and SCN2A, in human neuron and cerebral-organoid models with reduced gene activity. The team reported restoration of gene-expression levels and improvement in several mutation-associated cellular phenotypes. The experiments were conducted in laboratory models, not in people receiving treatment.

The bigger picture is a possible strategy for conditions in which a functional copy of a gene is present but does not produce enough protein. Rather than cutting DNA, the method aims to turn up the existing gene’s activity through regulatory elements. It is scientifically meaningful because it addresses a specific molecular mechanism, but autism is heterogeneous, and gene or organoid changes cannot establish clinical benefit or safety in humans. Delivery, specificity and long-term effects remain major questions.

An in-body CRISPR therapy approaches a regulatory decision

Nature reported on 12 November that a CRISPR-based treatment for hereditary angioedema is approaching a US regulatory decision after follow-up data suggested durable effects years after a single administration. Unlike existing approved CRISPR therapies that require extracting and modifying a patient’s cells, this approach delivers editing machinery directly into the body. The developers’ follow-up data are not the same as an independent long-term safety assessment.

The bigger picture is the potential expansion of gene editing from specialised cell-processing procedures to medicines administered directly to patients. That might broaden access in some conditions, but it raises safety and monitoring questions because edits made inside the body cannot be individually screened before cells are returned. The product is not yet approved, and potential benefits for one rare genetic disorder do not establish suitability for other diseases.
