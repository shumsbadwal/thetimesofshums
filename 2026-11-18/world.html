WorldInternational securityStory 1 of 7

The Middle East conflict spreads to Yemen and Gulf shipping

A Saudi-led coalition launched major strikes across Houthi-held areas of Yemen, saying it targeted military facilities. Houthi authorities reported at least 17 deaths, including people killed at a factory in Hodeida. In parallel, Iran claimed to have struck a US base in the Gulf, while Bahrain and Kuwait reported aerial threats. These are reports from participants in an active conflict, and not every operational or casualty claim has been independently confirmed.

The wider significance is that the violence is no longer confined to a single front. The Strait of Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb are vital to energy supply and commercial shipping; attacks, even without a formal blockade, can increase shipping risk, insurance costs and the price of imported goods. India has particular exposure through energy imports and maritime employment. The story to watch is whether commercial passage becomes more disrupted and whether regional governments can contain further escalation.

WorldGeopoliticsStory 2 of 7

Xi and Putin gather in Shenzhen ahead of talks with Trump

Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin spoke at the APEC gathering in Shenzhen ahead of an expected trilateral meeting with US President Donald Trump. Xi criticised trade protectionism and Putin attacked Western sanctions. The proposed talks are expected to cover a range of contentious issues, including Ukraine, the conflict involving Iran, trade and arms control; their outcomes were not yet known at the edition cutoff.

The significance is not merely diplomatic theatre. These powers can influence security, trade and technology rules that affect countries outside the room. India is one such country, with relationships spanning the Quad, Russia and China. A leaders’ meeting should not be treated as a breakthrough by itself: an agreed policy, a practical concession, or a verifiable change in behaviour would be the consequential result.

WorldHumanitarian affairsStory 3 of 7

Denial of aid to children in conflicts is sharply higher in UN records

A Save the Children analysis of UN-verified incidents found 8,322 cases of denial of humanitarian assistance to children in 2025, compared with 509 in 2015. The occupied Palestinian territories accounted for the largest number in 2025, followed by other conflict zones. These are verified recorded incidents, not a full count of every instance of aid obstruction around the world.

The figures illustrate how civilians can be harmed not only by attacks but also by the loss of food, medicine and essential services. Even where humanitarian law is clear, access on the ground depends on belligerents, border controls, security and the capacity of relief groups. Changes in reporting and verification capacity complicate decade-long comparisons, but the scale of the documented increase is significant.

WorldTechnology & tradeStory 4 of 7

Nvidia suspends shipments of some US-approved AI chips to China

Nvidia says it is no longer shipping certain licensed Hopper-generation data-centre processors to China. Interim CEO Colette Kress disclosed about $2.4 billion in sales of the relevant licensed products in the quarter ended 25 October. The firm has not fully specified how long the shipment interruption will last or how much is attributable to government rules versus commercial demand.

The bigger story is the growing entanglement of AI infrastructure and national industrial policy. Washington is tightening controls on advanced computing, while Beijing wants to build competing domestic chip capabilities. For suppliers and data-centre operators, even a product cleared for sale can remain exposed to shifting policy and strategic competition. Lost shipments are not the same as an end to China’s access to computing, nor proof that controls are achieving all their objectives.

WorldInvestigationsStory 5 of 7

A Deutsche Bank document leak puts high-risk client controls under scrutiny

An investigative collaboration, including Newslaundry, examined more than 15 million leaked Deutsche Bank records. One report identifies about $582 million in suspicious payments associated with Jeffrey Epstein. The archive also contains internal risk assessments and correspondence about other wealthy and politically connected clients. Reuters said it could not immediately independently verify the full leaked archive; Deutsche Bank has pointed to improvements in controls and said much of the conduct described is historical.

The important question is whether banks can apply anti-financial-crime safeguards consistently when dealing with powerful or profitable customers. A flagged transaction is not proof of a crime, but ignored alerts or inadequate escalation can expose weaknesses in supervision. This is a long-form investigation worth reading directly, rather than reducing it to a definitive verdict on every person mentioned.

WorldUkraine · Energy securityStory 6 of 7

Russian strikes leave Kyiv struggling with power outages as winter approaches

Repeated Russian attacks on Ukraine’s energy infrastructure have left Kyiv enduring severe power interruptions as colder weather approaches. A Guardian report describes residents managing disrupted electricity and heat alongside growing criticism of Mayor Vitali Klitschko over the city’s preparations. Those criticisms concern preparedness and responsibility; they should not obscure that attacks on infrastructure are the immediate cause of much of the damage.

The larger issue is how energy systems become a front in a prolonged war. Electricity outages affect water pumping, hospitals, lifts, public transport and livelihoods, with the greatest burden often falling on people who cannot arrange private backup power. Repairs can restore service but repeated attacks can undo progress. The distinction worth watching is between damage caused by new strikes and preventable weaknesses in local preparedness—both require evidence, and assigning responsibility remains politically contested.

WorldAsia-Pacific · Military signallingStory 7 of 7

Russian strategic bombers fly across East Asia and land in Vietnam

Two Russian Tu-95 strategic bombers made an unusual long-range deployment to Da Nang, Vietnam, after a flight that prompted air-defence responses and concern in Japan and Taiwan. Reuters reported that Japan protested suspected Russian aircraft entering its territorial airspace in a related incident, while aircraft movements also drew scrutiny around Taiwan and South Korea. Reports of an aircraft entering an air-defence identification zone should not automatically be described as a violation of sovereign airspace: they are legally different.

The broader significance is strategic signalling ahead of meetings involving the Russian, Chinese and American leaders. Deploying long-range aircraft tests logistics, demonstrates reach and draws regional military attention without necessarily signalling an imminent attack. Analysts differ on how closely the route was coordinated politically and what Moscow intended to convey. The practical consequences depend on whether the deployment remains a one-off demonstration or becomes part of a sustained pattern of regional military operations.
