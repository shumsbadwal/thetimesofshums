Adani’s Satara pumped-storage project faces wildlife-permission and cost scrutiny

Maharashtra’s Forest Department issued a stop-work order at Adani Green Energy’s proposed 1,500 MW Tarali pumped-storage project in Satara district, citing missing wildlife clearance. Separately, an Indian Express investigation found sharply different project-cost figures in regulatory documents: ₹5,675 crore and ₹56.75 crore. Because wildlife-related deposits can depend on project cost, the discrepancy is material. Adani disputes claims that it deliberately reduced costs to avoid obligations and says the figures refer to different scopes.

Pumped storage can help balance renewable power, but its energy-system usefulness does not resolve local ecological and governance questions. What matters next is clarification of permits, valuation bases, conservation payments and the impact on nearby communities. Allegations and company responses must both be kept visible until the review reaches supported conclusions.

Maharashtra announces a sulphur dioxide emissions market for industry

Maharashtra has announced India’s first sulphur dioxide emissions-trading scheme, covering 104 facilities across 17 industrial sectors, with trading scheduled to begin on 1 December. The market is designed to set emission limits and allow firms to trade unused allowances. Policymakers have described a goal of cutting covered emissions over time; the outcome is not yet established.

A tradable-permit system could reduce pollution at lower economic cost if regulators establish a credible cap, accurately measure emissions and enforce compliance. Without those conditions, trading can become accounting rather than environmental improvement. The proper benchmark will be measured air-quality and emissions performance after implementation, not the ceremony announcing the scheme.

Maharashtra’s voter-list revision gets an extended timetable

The Election Commission has extended the schedule for the Special Intensive Revision of electoral rolls in Maharashtra. Enumeration forms are to be distributed and collected through 30 November, claims and objections handled by 15 December, and the final roll published on 22 December.

Cleaning voter lists can remove duplicates and outdated entries, but the process also affects the basic right of eligible voters to participate. The quality of notification, time available to correct errors and access to appeals matter as much as the eventual roll count. The additional time may help administration but does not itself settle questions about fairness.

Mumbai sees poor air quality and further SpiceJet disruptions

Mumbai’s air pollution was elevated at several monitoring stations, with reported AQI readings approaching 286 at Navy Nagar and exceeding 200 at Bandra Kurla Complex, Deonar and Chembur. The figures differ by location and time and should not be reduced to one all-city measurement. Separately, multiple SpiceJet flight cancellations and delays disrupted passengers at the city airport.

The pollution readings point to a continuing health and urban-management challenge affected by emissions, dust and weather. The aviation disruption raises separate concerns about airline operations and passengers’ access to timely information and remedies. These are two shorter local developments rather than one causal story.

Court-appointed panel demands accountable action on construction and road dust

The Bombay High Court-appointed High Power Committee has ordered municipal bodies and infrastructure agencies across the Mumbai Metropolitan Region to take time-bound steps to tackle pollution, with responsibility attached to named officials. Its directions address construction and demolition waste, mechanised road cleaning, unpaved highway shoulders and enforcement against visibly polluting vehicles. The panel criticised reliance on paperwork without effective action on pollution sources.

The bigger picture is that Mumbai’s air-quality problem is an implementation and accountability issue as well as a technical one. Road shoulders and active construction sites can continually reintroduce dust even when streets are swept. The meaningful tests will be measurable reductions at source, independent monitoring and consequences for missed deadlines. The directive is an enforcement intervention, not proof that cleaner air has already been achieved.

BMC’s road-washing proposal raises questions about dust control and water use

Mumbai’s civic administration has submitted a proposal to hire 213 water tankers for a 150-day road-washing campaign intended to reduce dust. The revised plan bars the use of drinking water, according to Times of India reporting. The proposal was being put before the Standing Committee for approval; it should not be read as an already completed contract or as verified evidence that washing is reducing pollution.

The bigger issue is how to choose effective air-pollution interventions without creating new resource pressures. Road washing can address resuspended dust but its benefits depend on where and how often it is used, whether construction sources are controlled, and the availability and treatment quality of non-potable water. The scheme deserves evaluation against other measures, such as paving dusty verges and enforcing construction-site controls, rather than merely counting tanker trips.
