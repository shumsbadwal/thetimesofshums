Climate & EnvironmentClimate diplomacyStory 1 of 10

COP31 negotiators remain divided over fossil fuels and finance

Climate negotiations in Antalya have entered a contentious stage, with debate over language on the energy transition, finance and responsibilities between wealthier and developing economies. The first draft is not a final agreement; the conference is scheduled to run until 20 November.

The dispute is partly about distributing the costs of change. Fossil-fuel exporters, fast-growing economies and wealthy industrialised nations face different trade-offs and historical responsibilities. Agreement on high-level climate ambitions is much easier than agreement on who must reduce which emissions, how quickly, and who supplies the money and technology.

Climate & EnvironmentEnergy transitionStory 2 of 10

A voluntary pledge seeks 35% electrification of final energy use by 2035

A group of countries has endorsed a COP31 ambition to raise electricity’s share of global final energy demand from roughly 23% today to 35% in 2035. The agreement is voluntary and does not by itself specify how the extra power should be generated.

Electrifying vehicles, buildings and some industrial processes can improve efficiency and reduce direct fossil-fuel use. Climate benefits depend on the electricity source, grid capacity and investment in storage and infrastructure. The 35% target is a political ambition, not a prediction of what will happen or proof of low-carbon supply.

Climate & EnvironmentClimate financeStory 3 of 10

UNEP warns the adaptation-finance gap remains enormous

The UNEP Adaptation Gap Report 2026 estimates that developing countries may need about $305 billion annually for adaptation by 2035. Reported international public adaptation finance from developed to developing countries was $28.2–31.7 billion in 2024. The two numbers refer to different years and should be seen as an indication of scale, not an exact same-year shortfall.

Adaptation finances measures such as flood protection, heat response, coastal resilience and water security. Many highly exposed economies have limited fiscal capacity and contributed relatively little to historical emissions. Whether support is delivered as grants or loans can matter enormously for indebted countries. These are economic and institutional questions as well as climate questions.

Climate & EnvironmentAI & climateStory 4 of 10

A COP31 pledge on AI and climate lacks major US signatories at launch

The COP31 presidency and the International Telecommunication Union launched the Antalya AI Pledge to encourage more efficient computing and lower-carbon electricity for AI. Reuters reported that several leading US technology companies were absent from its initial signatory list, while Alibaba was among supporters.

The important test is not participation in a voluntary statement but measured changes in electricity use, carbon emissions, infrastructure design and reporting quality. Rapid data-centre investment may strain power systems, and energy efficiency gains can be offset by growth in total demand. Not signing is not proof of inaction, and signing is not proof of achieved reductions.

Climate & EnvironmentCarbon markets · IntegrityStory 5 of 10

Australia faces scrutiny over carbon credits for alleged “phantom forests”

Researchers have challenged Australian carbon-credit projects that claim gains from forest regrowth in semi-arid areas, arguing that some credited vegetation may not represent the durable forest increases assumed by the programme. The Guardian reported these allegations on 18 November, including concerns about credits potentially used to offset fossil-fuel emissions. The findings are a contested assessment, not a final legal ruling that every implicated credit is invalid.

The bigger picture is that carbon markets only reduce net atmospheric pollution if the claimed reductions or removals are real, additional, accurately measured and sufficiently durable. A method that overestimates vegetation growth could create paper credits without matching atmospheric benefit. This is closely relevant to marine CDR: the details differ, but both systems must show credible counterfactual baselines and independent verification rather than trusting project claims.

Climate & EnvironmentEnergy transition · Land and communitiesStory 6 of 10

The land footprint of the clean-energy transition is becoming a governance issue

A new International Land Coalition and Land Matrix assessment, reported by Mongabay in November, estimates that concessions linked to renewable-energy projects and critical transition minerals span about 328 million hectares worldwide—roughly the area of India. Much of the mapped area consists of projects still in exploration or planning, not completed installations. The report also identifies substantial overlap with land used by Indigenous Peoples and local communities.

The bigger picture is that decarbonisation reduces the need to burn fossil fuels but does not remove the environmental and social costs of extracting minerals and building energy infrastructure. Project area, actual land disturbance and permanent land conversion are different measures and should not be conflated. Planning therefore needs stronger consent, land-tenure protections and scrutiny of competing land uses, rather than treating every nominally green project as automatically benign.

Climate & EnvironmentObserved temperatures · CopernicusStory 7 of 10

Copernicus records the warmest October globally, with 2026 on course for exceptional heat

Copernicus Climate Change Service reports that October 2026 was the warmest October in the ERA5 reanalysis record, with a global-average surface air temperature of 15.35°C. That was 0.90°C above the October average for 1991–2020, and an estimated 1.75°C above the 1850–1900 pre-industrial reference. It exceeded the prior October record, set in 2023, by approximately 0.05°C on the ERA5 measure.

The wider picture is a planet experiencing persistent, exceptional warmth, not just an isolated hot day. The average for November 2025–October 2026 was 1.51°C above the estimated pre-industrial level. Copernicus estimates that January–October 2026 was the second warmest such interval on record and forecasts that the full calendar year is very likely to average above 1.5°C on its dataset. That forecast uses a statistical model for the remaining months and is not a completed year’s measurement.

These figures are based on ERA5 reanalysis, which integrates observations with a numerical weather model. Comparing its 1.75°C estimate against the pre-industrial baseline is not the same comparison as its 0.90°C anomaly relative to 1991–2020. Nor does one month or one year above 1.5°C automatically establish that the Paris Agreement’s long-term temperature limit has definitively been breached.

Climate & EnvironmentOcean temperatures · NOAA & CopernicusStory 8 of 10

Global sea-surface warmth sets another October record across major climate datasets

NOAA’s October 2026 climate assessment reports a global ocean-surface temperature anomaly of 1.14°C above its 20th-century reference, its highest monthly departure for any calendar month in the agency’s record. It was the fifth consecutive month to establish a record-high ocean-temperature departure for its respective month. Separately, Copernicus reports that sea-surface temperatures across the extra-polar ocean (60°S–60°N) averaged 20.95°C in October, also an October record in ERA5.

The bigger picture is the persistence of anomalous ocean warmth, which affects marine ecosystems, weather patterns and the transfer of heat and moisture between ocean and atmosphere. Warm surface waters can intensify some marine heatwaves, change the conditions experienced by fisheries and corals, and interact with El Niño-driven circulation changes. The exact environmental effect depends on region, depth, duration and local ecosystems—not the global average alone.

The NOAA anomaly and Copernicus temperature should not be plotted as if they are a like-for-like pair: they use different geographical coverage, reference periods and methods. A sea-surface record also does not by itself measure changes in deep-ocean heat content. For marine CDR, regional thermal structure and mixing may influence gas exchange, but these records do not quantify any specific project’s carbon-removal performance.

Climate & EnvironmentSea ice · NSIDC & CopernicusStory 9 of 10

Antarctic sea ice reaches the second-lowest October extent in satellite records

The National Snow and Ice Data Center estimates that Antarctic sea ice averaged 16.34 million km² in October 2026, the second-lowest October in its 48-year satellite record after 2023. Copernicus, using the EUMETSAT OSI SAF data product, independently reports the same ranking with an October average of 16.8 million km², about 9.3% below its 1991–2020 October average. The absolute values differ because the monitoring products and processing methods are not identical.

Conditions were particularly unusual in the Amundsen–Bellingshausen Sea sector and parts of the Southern Ocean’s Indian Ocean sector. NSIDC notes that weather patterns, including cyclonic activity, may help explain the regional distribution of sea ice. Copernicus reports that the Amundsen–Bellingshausen sector alone registered an October extent around 38% below average in its dataset.

The broader significance is that low Antarctic ice cover can change the exchange of heat and momentum between sea and atmosphere, affect light conditions and marine habitats, and influence exposure of ocean surfaces to weather. Arctic sea ice behaved differently: NSIDC ranked the Arctic October 2026 extent 12th lowest, at 6.69 million km². It would therefore be inaccurate to claim both poles were at their second-lowest October. A single month also cannot fully resolve the long-term causes of Antarctic variability.

Climate & EnvironmentAtmospheric composition · NOAAStory 10 of 10

NOAA estimates global atmospheric CO₂ near 428 ppm in mid-November

NOAA’s Global Monitoring Laboratory estimated the global atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration at approximately 428.30 parts per million for 16 November 2026 in its daily global-trend series, which was updated on 17 November. For comparison, about 280 ppm is commonly used as a representative pre-industrial atmospheric CO₂ level. The new estimate therefore describes a concentration more than 50% higher than that reference.

The bigger picture is that atmospheric concentration tracks the accumulated balance of emissions and removals, not simply the flow of carbon dioxide released during any one month. Even if growth in annual emissions slows, concentrations can continue to rise so long as net additions to the atmosphere remain positive. A CO₂ value for a given day is not an emissions figure, and it should not be interpreted as proof of any particular country’s recent performance.

NOAA’s global daily number is an estimate derived from background measurements and smoothed trends across observing stations, not a direct simultaneous measurement of every location on Earth. Short-term and seasonal variability should be considered separately from the long-term upward trend. For CDR, the atmospheric benchmark helps explain the scale of the challenge, but it cannot establish the effect of a small project without project-specific attribution and verification.
