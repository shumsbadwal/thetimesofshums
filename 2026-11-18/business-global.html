Nvidia’s $115.8bn quarter shows the scale of the AI infrastructure build-out

Nvidia reported $115.8 billion in quarterly revenue for the period ended 25 October, up 103% from a year earlier, and forecast a further strong quarter. The report came after Jensen Huang stepped down as chief executive, with interim leadership seeking to reassure investors amid the transition.

The numbers demonstrate continuing expenditure on AI processors and data-centre infrastructure, which also requires electricity, cooling, networking and capital. They do not establish that every AI application or customer investing in chips will earn adequate long-term returns. The distinction between the profitability of selling AI infrastructure and the benefits from using it remains central.

ECB officials may debate doubling banks' minimum reserves next week

Four sources told Reuters that European Central Bank policymakers are preparing to discuss a proposal on 25 November to raise the minimum unremunerated reserve requirement from 1% to 2% of qualifying liabilities. Reuters estimated that the change could save the Eurosystem about €4.4 billion annually at prevailing interest rates. It is a reported proposal, not an adopted rule.

Requiring banks to hold more non-interest-bearing reserves can affect liquidity and profitability as well as the ECB's own balance sheet. The practical impact will depend on the details, banks' funding positions and whether policymakers agree. Markets should distinguish the discussion from a decision to change policy rates.

Japan’s economy slips as household spending weakens

Preliminary government figures show Japan’s output contracted at an annualised rate of about 0.1% in July–September. Private consumption fell by 0.3% quarter-on-quarter, even while business investment remained positive. Consumer budgets have been pressured by higher prices.

Japan’s policymakers are trying to respond to inflation without undermining a fragile recovery. Weak household consumption alongside capital investment suggests companies and consumers are experiencing different conditions. A marginal quarterly fall does not prove a sustained recession, and preliminary GDP numbers may be revised.

Oil prices retreat despite renewed risks around the Strait of Hormuz

Brent crude fell to around $92 a barrel on the morning of 18 November after industry figures indicated US crude inventories rose 5.28 million barrels in the week to 13 November. Yet tanker attacks and the US–Iran confrontation continued to disrupt traffic around the Strait of Hormuz. Reuters noted that shipping data did not yet support treating regional flows as normal.

The tension between inventories and disruption helps explain why oil prices can fall even during a dangerous conflict. Physical stocks, expected production and demand affect the market alongside shipping risk. For India, lower international crude prices can ease import costs, but an increase in freight, insurance or disruption could offset some benefits. Reported prices are a timestamped market snapshot, not a closing price or a forecast.

Anthropic’s planned IPO becomes a test of AI valuations, not just AI enthusiasm

Anthropic is expected to price its initial public offering on 18 November US time, with its publicly filed range implying a valuation as high as approximately $1.4 trillion. The company’s prospectus also describes a private placement of $26 billion to a group of strategic technology investors at the offering price. As of this edition, the final IPO price and actual first-day market performance had not been established.

The bigger picture is a debate about how to value fast-growing AI businesses with huge computing commitments. A high public valuation would demonstrate investor appetite, but not prove that long-term revenue will justify the associated infrastructure costs. The IPO also exposes a complicated ecosystem in which chip suppliers, cloud firms and AI developers invest in one another. It is important to distinguish committed capital, revenue forecasts and realised profits rather than treating them as interchangeable.

The ECB warns that France’s debt and hedge-fund leverage could magnify shocks

The European Central Bank’s 18 November Financial Stability Review highlighted vulnerabilities in highly indebted governments, including France. Reuters reported French public debt at about 119% of GDP and a projected deficit of 5.5%. The ECB warned that some leveraged hedge-fund positions in bond markets could exacerbate abrupt repricing; only a handful of funds dominate certain interest-rate derivatives markets.

The bigger picture is how fiscal pressures, higher borrowing costs and non-bank finance interact. Bond markets can encourage governments to address deficits, but sudden selling can also create self-reinforcing instability when leveraged investors unwind positions. The ECB also noted competition for funding from US technology companies financing AI investment. These risks do not mean a sovereign default is imminent, but they merit attention beyond headline equity indexes.

Malaysia's third-quarter growth beats estimates as investment in AI infrastructure continues

Malaysia's economy expanded 5.5% year-on-year in the third quarter, above the 5.3% forecast cited by Reuters. Its central bank expects investment linked to artificial intelligence and data centres to continue supporting activity into 2027, while acknowledging uncertainty around global demand and new US controls on access to advanced computing.

Data-centre construction can lift exports, employment and capital spending, but creates questions about electricity, water, grid investment and how many long-term jobs remain once facilities are built. Malaysia's stronger GDP figure does not eliminate exposure to global technology cycles.

Tencent says US restrictions could slow AI model training without derailing cloud revenue

Tencent said new US limits on overseas remote access to advanced AI chips could slow training of its next model, but it did not expect a major near-term effect on its cloud revenue. The comments came as the Chinese technology company reported quarterly results amid broader competition in games, advertising, cloud and AI.

A constraint on high-end computing can have different consequences for research, customer services and ordinary cloud operations. Tencent's assessment is a corporate forecast rather than an independent guarantee. The wider issue for investors is how export controls change the costs and geography of AI infrastructure.

Luxury shares show how volatile technology wealth can spill into consumption

Luxury groups including LVMH, Hermès and Kering faced pressure as investors worried that weakness in AI-linked equities could curb spending among affluent American buyers. Reuters reported that luxury brands are leaning on US consumers to counter softer demand in China, adding sensitivity to stock-market swings.

A fall in luxury shares is not proof that wealthy consumers have already stopped shopping. The mechanism investors fear is a wealth effect: asset-price losses changing discretionary spending. Company sales disclosures over coming quarters will be a stronger test than a single market move.
