NOAA records extraordinary October global and ocean warmth

NOAA NCEI reports October 2026 global surface temperature at +1.43°C relative to its 20th-century October average, the largest monthly anomaly in its 1850–2026 record. Global ocean surface temperature was +1.14°C above its reference average, also an all-month anomaly record.

These are global monthly estimates, not a local weather reading. NOAA's dataset and baseline differ from ERA5; ocean heat content and surface sea temperature are also different variables.

Copernicus measures warmest October and forecasts annual 1.5°C exceedance

Copernicus ERA5 estimates October's global mean surface air temperature at 15.35°C, +0.90°C versus 1991–2020 and +1.75°C versus 1850–1900. Its statistical model puts 2026's annual anomaly around 1.56–1.61°C above pre-industrial levels.

The October figures are reanalysis estimates; the annual range is a forecast using observations through October. One calendar year above 1.5°C is not the Paris Agreement's long-term threshold.

WMO expects strong El Niño and positive Indian Ocean Dipole

The WMO's October seasonal update projected a strong Niño 3.4 anomaly near +3.7°C for October–December and a positive Indian Ocean Dipole seasonal mean around +0.5°C.

These are ensemble forecasts, not observed final seasonal values. Their relevance to South Asian rainfall and marine conditions depends on regional atmospheric response and forecast uncertainty.

Climate & EnvironmentReportingStory 4 of 6

Antarctic October sea ice ranks second-lowest in NOAA's satellite-era record

NOAA NCEI reports that Antarctic sea-ice extent during October 2026 was 730,000 square miles below the 1991–2020 mean, ranking second-lowest for October in the 48-year series. Combined global sea ice ranked third-lowest for the month.

Sea-ice extent is a satellite-derived area measure, not thickness, volume or land-ice mass. One month's ranking is meaningful in context of the multidecadal record but does not by itself identify a particular cause.

Climate & EnvironmentReportingStory 6 of 6

Cyclone Akvan underscores the distinction between hazard forecasts and climate attribution

IMD's forecast placed severe cyclonic storm Akvan on a track toward the Andhra Pradesh–Odisha coast with possible landfall on 20 November. The immediate public-safety concern is wind, rainfall and coastal exposure, not a premature claim about the storm's climate cause.

Warmer oceans can influence cyclone behaviour, but attributing any individual storm's intensity or trajectory to climate change requires a dedicated analysis. Forecast bulletins and long-term climate assessments answer different questions.
